# Election outcome, divided government, and confounders

**Bundle**: 02 — election-outcome-divided-government
**Parent question**: Is there a pattern in how the stock market goes before and after US midterms; what if both chambers flip against the president's party?

## Questions addressed
- Market returns after midterms by outcome (House/Senate flips: 1946, 1954, 1994, 2006, 2010, 2018, 2022)
- Unified vs divided government returns; gridlock research
- Counter-evidence and confounders; is the post-midterm rally outcome-independent?

*Quick mode: 7 search/fetch operations. Secondary sources only (no primary index data pulled). Several figures come from search-engine summaries of pages I did not read in full; flagged below.*

## Findings

### 1. Returns after midterms, by outcome
- **The post-midterm rally is outcome-independent in the data I found.** One-year S&P 500 return after midterms averages ~15.4% since 1950, with "not a single negative one-year return" ([Capital Group, accessed 2026-10-07](https://www.capitalgroup.com/institutions/global-consultants/en/insights/articles/how-us-midterm-elections-affect-the-markets.html)). BNY gives 16.6% average 12-month return since the 1950s, "regardless of results" ([BNY Investments, May 2026](https://www.bny.com/content/im/us/en/individual/articles/markets-and-economy/how-do-midterm-elections-impact-markets-over-time.html)). Another summary cites 5.8% / 10.5% / 14.8% at 3 / 6 / 12 months across 25 midterms ([search summary of RBC/Yardeni-type tables, 2026-10-07](https://www.rbcwealthmanagement.com/en-us/insights/midterms-the-market-and-what-matters)). Streak counts differ by source: 18 straight positive periods since 1954 ([Fool/LPL-sourced, Jan 2026](https://www.fool.com/investing/2026/01/12/why-history-isnt-on-the-side-of-the-sp-500-heading)) vs 19 since 1942. Avg figures range 14.8-18.2% depending on start date and window. Medium conviction on the range, high on "never negative" as a claim repeated by several sources (all likely tracing to the same dataset, so not independent).
- **Flip cases (president's party lost the House), 12 months after election** (S&P price returns, from a search summary of [Indexology/S&P DJI](https://www.indexologyblog.com/2018/11/10/stocks-rocked-the-house-post-midterm-elections/) and [Yardeni](https://archive.yardeni.com/pub/cc_20221109.pdf) tables; I did not open these pages, so treat the numbers as medium-low confidence):

| Election | 12-mo S&P return |
|---|---|
| Nov 1994 (R takes House+Senate, Clinton) | +25.9% |
| Nov 2006 (D takes House+Senate, Bush) | +6.6% |
| Nov 2010 (R takes House, Obama) | +2.1% (looks low; I recall about +4-5% price-only. Verify) |
| Nov 2018 (D takes House, Trump) | +12.0% |
| Nov 2022 (R takes House, Biden) | +13.6% |

  All five positive, range +2% to +26%, simple mean ~12%, i.e. at or somewhat below the all-midterm average. n=5, so no basis for claiming a flip is better or worse than a non-flip. Of these, **only 1994 and 2006 flipped both chambers**; 2010, 2018 and 2022 flipped one. Sample for "both flip" is therefore 2 post-1990 (plus 1946, 1954 which I did not get numbers for: unknown; 1946 was a Republican sweep under Truman, 1954 Democrats took both under Eisenhower; both are in the "never negative" universe per Capital Group but I have no individual figures).
- **Pre-election pattern**: midterm years average ~4.6-4.7% S&P return vs 9.5% in other years (1930-2022, [Capital Group](https://www.capitalgroup.com/institutions/global-consultants/en/insights/articles/how-us-midterm-elections-affect-the-markets.html)); average peak-to-trough drawdown 17.5% since 1950, weakness typically bottoming around September ([Fool/LPL summary, Jan 2026](https://www.fool.com/investing/2026/01/12/why-history-isnt-on-the-side-of-the-sp-500-heading)). One source says 12 of 17 midterm years since 1957 saw a 10% correction and 6 a bear market, though that seems high ([KuCoin blog, 2026](https://www.kucoin.com/blog/sp500-record-high-vix-low-midterm-market-correction); low-quality source, low conviction). Implication for the user's question: "spike until the election then drop" is **not** the pattern. The historical pattern is the reverse: weak/volatile into Q3 of midterm year, a low around late Sep-Oct, then rally after. The recent sharp rally fits "late-bottom then relief" better than "pre-election run-up."

### 2. Unified vs divided government
- Pre-2010 work: S&P gained 7.6% annualized in "total unity" 1900-Oct 2010 vs 6.8% all years; only **2.0% annualized in 12 years of split House/Senate**; 6.8% in 32 years of partial gridlock ([Kiplinger / Wealth Management summary, 2010](https://www.wealthmanagement.com/wealth-management-industry-trends/political-gridlock-is-not-in-fact-good-for-markets)). Post-WWII: 10.7% under total unity vs 3.5% under total gridlock (same source). A Journal of Portfolio Management study (1926-2000) found large caps ~0.8 pt/yr lower in gridlock, small caps worse, and divided government associated with ~2.8 pt higher annual volatility (same search results, 2010-era articles).
- Counter-claim: Capital Group says markets averaged **double-digit returns** under unified, divided and opposition-controlled Congress since 1933 ([Capital Group](https://www.capitalgroup.com/institutions/global-consultants/en/insights/articles/how-us-midterm-elections-affect-the-markets.html)). Another summary says a Democratic president with GOP/split Congress has been a historically strong combination, while a Republican president with Democratic Congress is mixed ([First Citizens, 2022](https://www.firstcitizensgroup.com/bb/2022/09/26/us-mid-terms-and-the-impact-on-the-market/); search summary only).
- Reconciliation: the sources disagree because of window and definition (full-sample "gridlock" vs "years after midterms"). Rule of thumb for the user: academic/older work leans mildly **negative** on true House/Senate split; practitioner (sell-side/asset-manager) work leans **neutral-to-positive**. Neither shows a causal channel; party control explains little next to macro.

### 3. Confounders and counter-evidence
- **Outcome-independence is partly survivorship of the macro cycle.** Midterm years are the weak year of the 4-year cycle and the post-midterm 12 months coincide with the strong pre-election year; the rally may be cycle/uncertainty-resolution, not electoral content ([BNY, May 2026](https://www.bny.com/content/im/us/en/individual/articles/markets-and-economy/how-do-midterm-elections-impact-markets-over-time.html) attributes it to uncertainty resolution). Cannot separate these with the sources I pulled. Unknown.
- **Specific confounders**: 1974 (post-Watergate, oil shock, recession, bear market bottoming Dec 1974; the post-election year was nonetheless positive per the "never negative" claim); 2000-02 (not a midterm election-year drop of note in my sources; 2002 midterm Nov was near the Oct 2002 low and rallied after: bear-market-low timing); 2022 (Fed tightening drove the drawdown; Oct 2022 low preceded the Nov election). I did not source individual figures for these: mostly from background knowledge, low-medium conviction. The recurring point: lows often occur **before** the election (1982, 2002, 2022, plus 1962 and 1974-ish), so the "post-election rally" is partly a late-bottom artefact.
- **Overlap in sources**: nearly all "never negative" statements derive from the same Stock Trader's Almanac/S&P series; treat as one data point, n≈19-20.
- **2026 context** (VERIFY items): Prediction markets give Democrats ~85% to flip the House and Republicans ~51% to hold the Senate ([DeFiRate, 2026](https://defirate.com/news/democrats-85-percent-to-flip-house-2026-midterms-senate-still-toss-up/); date not confirmed, low-medium conviction), which implies the user's premise (GOP trifecta currently) is consistent but I did not directly confirm current seat counts. A full flip of both chambers is thus perhaps ~40-45% on those odds (my arithmetic, assuming correlation; not sourced). S&P hit a record 7,798.99 on 2026-08-13 per a search summary, YTD ~+13% ([ROIC/Reuters poll, 2026-08-26](https://www.roic.ai/news/wall-streets-2026-target-sp-500-seen-hitting-7900-08-26-2026)); Brooke Thackray (Horizons) is framing midterms as a buying opportunity ([BNN Bloomberg, 2026-09-03](https://www.bnnbloomberg.ca/investing/opinion/2026/09/03/us-midterm-elections-set-to-bring-a-buying-opportunity-brooke-thackray/)). Note: this conflicts with "typical Q3 weakness"; this year's market seems to have bucked the pattern, so a historical bottom-then-rally template may not apply if no drawdown occurred.

## Takeaways for a long-term holder
1. No historical evidence of a "spike to election, then drop." The seasonal tendency is opposite (soft into autumn, rally after).
2. Outcome (flip vs not, one vs both chambers) has not mattered much for the next 12 months in the sample (all positive); n is too small to rank outcomes. Divided government may add volatility and slightly lower long-run returns in the older literature.
3. Macro (Fed, recession, valuation) dominates; 12-month "always positive" is a small-n, correlated-source statistic and shouldn't be relied on as a guarantee.
